The water cycle describes how water moves between the oceans, the atmosphere and the land. Heat from the sun evaporates water from the surface of seas and lakes, and plants release more water vapour through their leaves. As the moist air rises it cools, and the vapour condenses into tiny droplets that form clouds. When the droplets grow large enough, they fall as rain, snow or hail. Some of this water runs off into rivers and streams, some soaks into the ground and refills aquifers, and some is frozen for long periods in glaciers and ice sheets. Over thousands of years, a single molecule of water may pass through every part of this cycle many times.
The printing press, developed in Europe in the middle of the fifteenth century, changed how information spread. Before it, books were copied by hand, which made them rare and expensive. Movable metal type allowed a workshop to produce hundreds of identical pages in a day. Within fifty years, printing shops had opened in more than two hundred cities, and millions of books were in circulation. Cheaper books encouraged literacy, helped standardise spelling, and allowed scientists and scholars to share and check each other's work far more quickly than before.
